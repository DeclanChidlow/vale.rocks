There is a common misconception that colour blindness means only seeing in greyscale. This can be the case with some forms of colour blindness, but is rare, and it is much more common to only have some colour perception impacted. Human eyes work with rod and cone cells. Rods largely handle peripheral vision and low-light vision, while cones handle vision in well-lit situations and colour perception.

Humans have three classes of cones (trichromacy), each of which is sensitive to a different wavelength of visible light. L-cones are most sensitive to green through yellow to red, M-cones to green through yellow, and S-cones to blue. There is overlap between what colour each cone sees -- especially the M and L cones.

Below are _demonstratory_ figures of the effects of various colour blindness. They do not represent all people's experiences with colour blindness and are representations only. Complete blindness (ending in _-opia_) is shown here, though partial cone dysfunction (ending in _-omaly_, like protanomaly or deuteranomaly) is not. The majority of people with colour blindness are have only partial colour deficiency.

Achromatopsia and blue-cone monochromacy especially are usually paired with severe photosensitivity and reduced vision. Globally, it is estimated that \~4.5% of males and \~0.6% of females are colour blind. Ethnicity has a large influence on these figures. Red-green colour blindness is the most common by a significant margin (\~95% of all colour blindness).

Remember, no two people's eyes are exactly the same. Different people have different vision and might have colour vision deficiency paired with other conditions.
